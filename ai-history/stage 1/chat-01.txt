● I read the brief (the docx) and both classes. Short answer: keep them separate
  — this split is good, and the brief actively rewards it. Here's why.

  Why the split works

  The brief's first learning outcome is "assign clear responsibilities to 
  objects." Your two classes have crisply different responsibilities:

  ┌────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────┐
  │                │            Responsibility            │   Depends on    │
  ├────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────┤
  │                │ Player-facing: validates manager     │ Choices, staff, │
  │ GameController │ choices, executes waiter/chef        │  menu,          │
  │                │ actions, guards turn lifecycle       │ GameState       │
  ├────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────┤
  │                │ Automatic simulation: eating,        │ Only GameState  │
  │ TurnProcessor  │ payment, satisfaction decay,         │ + Cashier       │
  │                │ abandonment                          │                 │
  └────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────┘

  Two things in the brief make the separation pay off concretely:

  1. Stage 2's explicit rule: "Different behaviour must be dispatched through 
     the customer objects, not through a chain of type checks in the turn 
     controller." Because your end-of-turn logic lives in TurnProcessor and just
     calls customer.decreaseSatisfaction() / cashier.processPayment(), VIP and
     Critic later slot in via polymorphic overrides with zero changes to turn
     flow. If this logic were fused into the controller, that requirement would
     be much easier to accidentally violate. Your current design is clean
     evidence of that requirement for the report.
  2. Stage 3 extensions drop in without touching the controller. Host
     (auto-seats one per turn) and Happy Hour (satisfaction recovery) are
     automatic game-loop changes — they belong beside
     processEating/processPayments, not among player-choice validation.
     TurnProcessor gives them a natural home.

  Merging them would give you a ~350-line controller doing two unrelated jobs
  (interactive command handling + simulation tick), which is exactly the kind of
  thing markers flag.

  Two small observations (not reasons to merge)

  - Asymmetry in automatic logic: arrivals are automatic too, but they run in
    GameController.processArrivals() (GameController.java:273) while all other
    automatic logic is in TurnProcessor. In Stage 3, Happy Hour needs a
    start-of-turn automatic step anyway. Consider renaming runEndOfTurn into a
    turn engine with runStartOfTurn(...) (arrivals) and runEndOfTurn(...) — then
    TurnProcessor is the single home for everything the manager doesn't decide,
    and the controller is purely validation + delegation. That's a nice story
    for the stage-1 → stage-3 design-evolution section too, if you don't want to
    change it now.
  - The instanceof dispatch over WaiterChoice subtypes is fine — the brief's "no
    type-check chains" rule is about customer behavior, and it also says don't
    invent interfaces just for a checklist. I'd leave it.

  One terminology note for your report: calling TurnProcessor a control class
  handling automatic game-loop steps and GameController the boundary-facing
  orchestrator matches the "entity / control / boundary" framing the
  design-evidence section asks you to annotate.
